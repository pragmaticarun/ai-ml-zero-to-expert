Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)

β in DPO. In the RLHF objective β is the KL weight. In the DPO loss it is the scale on log-ratios inside the sigmoid. Larger β means a given log-prob change produces a bigger margin, so the loss saturates sooner and the gradient weight σ(−margin) drops sooner. The policy therefore moves less from π_ref. Smaller β lets the policy move further before the loss is satisfied. That is the same direction as in Day 106: big β = stay close.

Learning rate interacts. With LoRA at lr 5e-4 (Day 108) the optimiser can satisfy the loss in a few steps by drastic changes. Lower lr changes the path, not only the speed: a small step size finds changes closer to the reference.

Forgetting. Preference tuning can damage abilities that the pairs do not cover. A cheap proxy is perplexity on held-out general text. We reuse Week 15's Shakespeare validation text: 8 windows of 256 tokens, on which the instruct model scored 32.05 (Week 15 log day104_eval_finetuned.log).

Measuring movement. Three numbers tell you how far the policy moved: Δlog-prob of chosen and rejected on held-out pairs, the change in general perplexity, and sample outputs. A full KL estimate would require sampling from the policy; Δlog-probs on the pairs are a cheap substitute.

Both lr 1e-4 runs trained in 35-36 s and reached held-out preference accuracy 1.00. With β = 0.5 the rejected answer moved only −10.3 nats instead of −81.2, exactly the "stay closer" effect, and the example output keeps all four words (wrong order, but well-formed). Perplexity barely moved from 32.05 in either case (32.35 and 32.14), so on this proxy neither run forgot much. Exact match is 0.20-0.23 on 40 prompts; Day 110 shows that such differences are within noise.

Block 4: Practice (1h)

  1. Why does larger β make the loss saturate sooner?
  2. Day 109 β = 0.5: margin 11.32 with Δ chosen +12.3 and Δ rejected −10.3. Check the arithmetic.
  3. Why is perplexity on Shakespeare only a proxy for forgetting?
  4. Which setting would you pick from the table, and what would you need to be sure?

Answers. (1) The margin is β times the log-ratio difference; the same log-prob change gives a β-times larger margin. (2) 0.5 × (12.3 − (−10.3)) = 0.5 × 22.6 = 11.3, matching 11.32. (3) It measures one text domain and only likelihood, not instruction following, safety or reasoning. (4) None of the exact-match differences is reliable on n = 40; β = 0.5 moves least and keeps outputs well-formed, so prefer it unless a larger test set shows otherwise.

Extra notes

Changing β and lr together is a common confounder (Week 15's LoRA vs full fine-tune comparison had the same problem). Change one thing at a time when you want to attribute an effect. Real runs also tune the number of epochs: DPO on a fixed dataset over-fits quickly, which in practice often shows as falling chosen log-probs.

Going deeper

  • Search for analyses of "DPO chosen log-probability decrease" to see this effect discussed in the literature, and check whether the paper's setting matches yours.
  • Read the IPO paper ("A General Theoretical Paradigm to Understand Learning from Human Preferences") for a loss that does not saturate in the same way.

Interview-style question (15 min)

"Your DPO run has 100% preference accuracy after 50 steps but the model got worse in human evals. What do you check?" Points: Δlog-probs of chosen (falling?), output samples, length change, general benchmarks or perplexity for forgetting, β and lr, number of epochs, the quality and diversity of the rejected answers, and the held-out set size.

Common mistakes

  • Tuning β while also changing the learning rate and steps.
  • Comparing exact-match numbers that differ by 1-2 items out of 40.
  • Not measuring anything outside the preference task.

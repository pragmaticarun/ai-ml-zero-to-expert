Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)

Why preferences? After pretraining a model predicts text; after supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on demonstrations it follows instructions. But many qualities we want (helpful, harmless, concise, honest) are easier to judge than to demonstrate. A labeller who cannot write the perfect answer can still say which of two answers is better. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) turns those comparisons into a training signal.

The classic three-stage pipeline. 1. SFT: fine-tune the pretrained model on demonstrations. This is the starting policy and usually also the reference policy π_ref. 2. Reward model (RM): collect pairs (prompt x, answer y_w preferred over y_l) and train a model r(x, y) that gives the preferred answer a higher score. 3. Policy optimisation: sample answers from the policy, score them with the RM, and update the policy to get higher reward, while a KL penalty keeps it close to π_ref. The usual optimiser is PPO (proximal policy optimisation).

Bradley-Terry. The standard link between rewards and preferences is P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l)), where σ is the logistic sigmoid. Training the RM is then logistic regression on reward differences: loss = −log σ(r_w − r_l). Two consequences: only differences matter, so the reward is defined only up to a constant per prompt; and the scale is set by how confident the labels are.

The KL-regularised objective. We maximise E_{y∼π}[r(x, y)] − β·KL(π ‖ π_ref). Without the KL term the policy would collapse onto whatever the RM over-rates, including its mistakes (reward hacking). β trades reward against staying close to a model we trust. For a fixed prompt this objective has a closed-form optimum: π*(y) ∝ π_ref(y)·exp(r(y)/β). Small β gives a nearly greedy policy; large β leaves you at π_ref. Remember this formula: DPO on Day 108 is derived from it.

PPO intuition. PPO estimates how much better than expected each sampled answer was (the advantage), raises the log-probability of good samples and lowers bad ones, and clips the update so the new policy does not move too far from the policy that collected the samples in one step. In LLM RLHF the reward is usually given once per full answer, and the KL penalty is added per token. A value network (critic) estimates expected reward to reduce variance. That makes four models in memory at once, which is why simpler methods (DPO, and critic-free methods like REINFORCE-style or group-relative baselines) became popular.

Block 2: Worked example (45 min)

code/week-16/day106_rlhf_toy.py has two parts, both pure torch and done in a few seconds.

Part 1: Bradley-Terry. A hidden linear reward r(x) = w·x with w = [2.0, −1.0, 0.5, 0.0, 1.5] generates 2,000 noisy preferences (sampled with P = σ(r_a − r_b)). Fitting w by logistic loss on x_a − x_b recovers it.

Part 2: KL-regularised bandit. Five arms with rewards [1.0, 0.8, 0.5, 0.2, 0.0] and a reference policy [0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.35] that prefers the bad arms. We compute π* for several β, then check that REINFORCE with a per-sample KL penalty converges to the same answer.

Verified: - BT fit: fitted w = [1.92, −1.0, 0.47, 0.05, 1.46]; on 1,000 new pairs it ranks pairs the same way as the true reward in 0.996 of cases; final train loss 0.299. - Adding +10 to every reward changes the BT loss by 0.0e+00: the constant is not identifiable. - β = 0.05: π* = [0.964, 0.035, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0], expected reward 0.993, KL to ref 2.816. - β = 0.2: π* = [0.45, 0.331, 0.148, 0.049, 0.021], reward 0.799, KL 1.193. - β = 1.0: π* = [0.097, 0.158, 0.235, 0.261, 0.249], reward 0.393, KL 0.053. - β = 5.0: π* = [0.058, 0.111, 0.208, 0.294, 0.33], reward 0.309, KL 0.002. - REINFORCE with KL penalty (β 0.2, 3,000 steps × 64 samples) ended at [0.45, 0.331, 0.148, 0.049, 0.021], equal to the closed form to three decimals.

Read the β table as the whole RLHF trade-off in one place: reward rises from 0.309 to 0.993 as β falls, and the price is KL rising from 0.002 to 2.816.

Block 4: Practice (1h)

  1. Show that σ(a) + σ(−a) = 1, and explain why that means BT treats (y_w, y_l) and (y_l, y_w) consistently.
  2. Derive π* ∝ π_ref·exp(r/β) by maximising Σ π(y) r(y) − β Σ π(y) log(π(y)/π_ref(y)) subject to Σ π = 1 (use a Lagrange multiplier).
  3. For β → 0 and β → ∞, what does π* become?
  4. Why does the BT loss not change when you add a constant to all rewards?

Answers. (1) σ(−a) = 1/(1+e^{a}) = 1 − σ(a); swapping the pair flips the sign of the difference, so the probabilities stay complementary. (2) Setting the derivative to zero gives r(y) − β(log(π/π_ref) + 1) − λ = 0, so π ∝ π_ref·exp(r/β). (3) β → 0: all mass on the best arm (argmax r); β → ∞: π_ref. (4) The loss uses only r_w − r_l; the constant cancels (measured 0.0e+00 change).

Extra notes

The bandit is a "one-step" problem: no sequence of tokens, no credit assignment. Real LLM RLHF is the same objective over whole answers, where π(y|x) is a product over tokens, so log π(y|x) is a sum of token log-probs. That sum is what Day 108 computes in logp_batch. The per-sample KL estimate used in part 2 (log π(y)/π_ref(y) for a sampled y) is the same estimator used per token in many LLM RLHF implementations.

Interview-style question (15 min)

"Why do RLHF pipelines include a KL penalty to the SFT model? What goes wrong if β is too small or too large?" A good answer covers: the RM is only accurate near the data it was trained on; without the KL the policy finds inputs where the RM is wrong (reward hacking) and drifts in style or fluency; too-large β means nothing changes (Day 106: β = 5.0 moves KL only 0.002); too-small β means near-greedy optimisation of an imperfect reward.

Common mistakes

  • Treating RM scores as absolute. They are only meaningful as differences for the same prompt.
  • Thinking the KL is a regulariser on the weights. It is on the output distributions.
  • Forgetting that RLHF does not add knowledge; it reshapes which behaviours the model prefers.
  • Calling any fine-tune on preference data "RLHF". DPO, for example, has no RL loop.

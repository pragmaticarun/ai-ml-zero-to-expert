Interactive weekly quiz (12 questions)

Q1.

Name the three stages of the classic RLHF pipeline.

SFT on demonstrations; reward model from pairwise preferences; policy optimisation (e.g. PPO) against the RM with a KL penalty to the SFT/reference model.

Q2.

Write the Bradley-Terry probability that y_w is preferred to y_l.

P = σ(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l)).

Q3.

Why is a reward model's output defined only up to a constant per prompt?

BT uses only reward differences, so adding a constant changes nothing (Day 106 measured a 0.0e+00 change in loss).

Q4.

What is the closed-form optimum of the KL-regularised objective for one prompt?

π*(y) ∝ π_ref(y)·exp(r(y)/β).

Q5.

In Day 106's bandit, what happened to expected reward and KL as β went from 5.0 to 0.05?

Expected reward rose from 0.309 to 0.993 and KL to the reference rose from 0.002 to 2.816.

Q6.

Why is the reward read from the last token in a causal-LM reward model?

In a causal model only the last position has attended to the entire prompt and answer.

Q7.

What did RM B (chosen always ending with "!") score when the "!" was moved to the wrong answer, and what does that show?

0.00 pairwise accuracy: it learned the planted shortcut rather than correctness (also only 0.62 with no "!" at all).

Q8.

Write the DPO loss.

L = −log σ(β[(log π_θ(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x)) − (log π_θ(y_l|x) − log π_ref(y_l|x))]).

Q9.

On Day 108, preference accuracy was 1.00 but exact match 0.17. Explain.

DPO satisfied the loss mainly by pushing the rejected answer down (Δ −126.7 nats held-out) while raising the chosen one only a little (+8.6); that does not make the correct answer the greedy output, and outputs degraded.

Q10.

What did increasing β from 0.1 to 0.5 do to Δlog-prob of the rejected answer on Day 109?

It shrank from −81.2 to −10.3 nats: larger β keeps the policy closer to the reference.

Q11.

Why was the DPO implicit reward a bad best-of-n scorer on Day 111?

It is a log-ratio trained only on chosen/rejected kinds of answers; on new samples it favoured ones the reference found very unlikely, scoring 0.00 vs 0.20 for the verifier.

Q12.

On Day 112, which objectives reached 0.70 exact match, and why did plain DPO not help on this task?

SFT on chosen and DPO+NLL both reached 0.70 (plain DPO 0.23). The chosen answers were exactly correct, so imitation already captured the full signal; the NLL term stopped DPO from merely lowering the rejected answer.

Week goals

  • Understand the RLHF pipeline: supervised fine-tuning, a reward model from pairwise preferences, then KL-regularised policy optimisation.
  • Fit a Bradley-Terry reward model, first on a toy problem and then on a small language model, and watch it learn a shortcut.
  • Derive and implement Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) from scratch on SmolLM2-135M-Instruct with LoRA.
  • See what β and the learning rate do, and why "preference accuracy = 1.00" does not mean the model is good.
  • Measure evaluation pitfalls: small test sets, prompt wording, metric choice, contamination.
  • Use inference-time selection (best-of-n) and see a reward being exploited.
  • Weekly project: SFT vs DPO vs DPO+NLL on the same data and budget.

Compute note (CPU vs GPU). All language-model work uses SmolLM2-135M(-Instruct), LoRA r=8 or r=16, batches of 4-8 pairs and 60-120 steps. One DPO step (4 pairs, so 8 sequences forward and backward) took about 0.6-0.7 s here (120 steps in 84 s; 60 steps in 35-36 s). That shows the mechanics and the failure modes. It does not reproduce real preference tuning, which uses tens of thousands of human or AI-labelled pairs, models of 1-70B parameters, and GPUs. On a GPU you would also run PPO properly (it needs a policy, a reference, a reward model and a value model in memory at once, plus sampling in the loop), which is not practical on this CPU.

Models and data. HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M and HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M-Instruct from the local Hugging Face cache (verified in Week 15). The preference data is synthetic and generated by the scripts: prompts "Reverse the order of these words: …" over a fixed 40-word vocabulary, 1,000 training prompts and 40 held-out test prompts (test prompts that also occur in training are removed). Chosen = the correctly reversed words; rejected = the words left in their original order (or a random other order where stated). Synthetic pairs make the "true" preference unambiguous, which is what we need to see the algorithms clearly; real preference data is noisy and subjective.

## Day 106: RLHF overview: preferences, Bradley-Terry and the KL-regularised objective
**Review today:** Day 105 (+1), Day 103 (+3), Day 99 (+7), Day 85 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Why preferences?** After pretraining a model predicts text; after supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on demonstrations it follows instructions. But many qualities we want (helpful, harmless, concise, honest) are easier to *judge* than to *demonstrate*. A labeller who cannot write the perfect answer can still say which of two answers is better. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) turns those comparisons into a training signal.

**The classic three-stage pipeline.**
1. *SFT*: fine-tune the pretrained model on demonstrations. This is the starting policy and usually also the *reference* policy π_ref.
2. *Reward model (RM)*: collect pairs (prompt x, answer y_w preferred over y_l) and train a model r(x, y) that gives the preferred answer a higher score.
3. *Policy optimisation*: sample answers from the policy, score them with the RM, and update the policy to get higher reward, while a KL penalty keeps it close to π_ref. The usual optimiser is PPO (proximal policy optimisation).

**Bradley-Terry.** The standard link between rewards and preferences is P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l)), where σ is the logistic sigmoid. Training the RM is then logistic regression on reward *differences*: loss = −log σ(r_w − r_l). Two consequences: only differences matter, so the reward is defined only up to a constant per prompt; and the scale is set by how confident the labels are.

**The KL-regularised objective.** We maximise E_{y∼π}[r(x, y)] − β·KL(π ‖ π_ref). Without the KL term the policy would collapse onto whatever the RM over-rates, including its mistakes (reward hacking). β trades reward against staying close to a model we trust. For a fixed prompt this objective has a closed-form optimum: π*(y) ∝ π_ref(y)·exp(r(y)/β). Small β gives a nearly greedy policy; large β leaves you at π_ref. Remember this formula: DPO on Day 108 is derived from it.

**PPO intuition.** PPO estimates how much better than expected each sampled answer was (the *advantage*), raises the log-probability of good samples and lowers bad ones, and clips the update so the new policy does not move too far from the policy that collected the samples in one step. In LLM RLHF the reward is usually given once per full answer, and the KL penalty is added per token. A value network (critic) estimates expected reward to reduce variance. That makes four models in memory at once, which is why simpler methods (DPO, and critic-free methods like REINFORCE-style or group-relative baselines) became popular.

Real LLM RLHF is the same objective over whole answers, where π(y|x) is a product over tokens, so log π(y|x) is a *sum* of token log-probs. That sum is what Day 108 computes in `logp_batch`. ### Interview-style question (15 min)
"Why do RLHF pipelines include a KL penalty to the SFT model? What goes wrong if β is too small or too large?" A good answer covers: the RM is only accurate near the data it was trained on; without the KL the policy finds inputs where the RM is wrong (reward hacking) and drifts in style or fluency; too-large β means nothing changes (Day 106: β = 5.0 moves KL only 0.002); too-small β means near-greedy optimisation of an imperfect reward.

### Common mistakes
- Treating RM scores as absolute. They are only meaningful as differences for the same prompt.
- Thinking the KL is a regulariser on the *weights*. It is on the output *distributions*.
- Forgetting that RLHF does not add knowledge; it reshapes which behaviours the model prefers.
- Calling any fine-tune on preference data "RLHF". DPO, for example, has no RL loop.

## Day 107: Reward modelling on a small LM, and shortcuts
**Review today:** Day 106 (+1), Day 104 (+3), Day 100 (+7), Day 86 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Architecture.** A reward model is usually the SFT model with its language-model head replaced by a *scalar head*: a linear layer from the final hidden state of the *last token* to one number. The whole prompt + answer is read, and the last position summarises it. Training uses the BT loss from Day 106 on (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples. In practice people fine-tune the full network or add LoRA; here we add LoRA r=8 to every linear layer of SmolLM2-135M plus a fresh head (2,442,817 trainable parameters).

**What the RM actually learns.** The RM learns whatever feature best separates chosen from rejected *in its training data*. If the data contain a spurious correlation (chosen answers are longer, more polite, use bullet points, end with "!"), the RM can learn that instead of the property you care about. During policy optimisation the policy then produces more of that feature: *reward hacking*. Length bias is the best-documented real example: human and AI raters tend to prefer longer answers, RMs pick this up, and optimised policies get longer.

**Generalisation.** An RM is only trusted near its training distribution. If training rejections are always of one kind, the RM may learn "detect that kind of mistake" rather than "detect correctness". A policy being optimised will quickly leave the training distribution, so this matters.

**Evaluating an RM.** The basic metric is held-out *pairwise accuracy*: the fraction of pairs where r(chosen) > r(rejected). Good practice also tests pairs built differently from the training pairs, and checks for known biases (length, format, sycophancy).

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
`code/week-16/day107_reward_model.py` trains two RMs for 120 steps × 8 pairs each with AdamW (lr 5e-4).

*RM A*: rejected = unreversed words. Tested on held-out prompts with (i) the same kind of rejection and (ii) a *random other order* as the rejection, which it never saw.

*RM B*: a planted shortcut. Every chosen training answer ends with "!", and rejected answers (random other orders) never do. Tested with the "!" as in training, with no "!" anywhere, and with the "!" moved to the wrong answer.

**Verified (out/day107_reward_model.log):**
- RM A trained in 90 s; last batch loss 0.000.
- RM A held-out accuracy: 1.00 with the training kind of rejection, but only 0.68 when the rejection is a random other order.
- Example rewards from RM A on one test prompt: correct 12.02, unreversed −4.59, correct + " Great question!" 9.29.
- RM B trained in 105 s; held-out accuracy 1.00 with "!" as in training, 0.62 with no "!" anywhere, and **0.00** when "!" is moved to the wrong answer.

So RM A partly learned "is this the unreversed order?" rather than "is this correctly reversed?", and RM B learned almost only the "!". A 135M model with 120 steps is a small RM, but the pattern (perfect in-distribution accuracy, poor out-of-distribution accuracy, shortcuts) is the general one.

### Extra notes
Real mitigations include more diverse rejected answers, training on pairs that differ in one feature only, ensembles of RMs, length-normalised or length-penalised rewards, and keeping the KL penalty. ## Day 108: DPO from scratch
**Review today:** Day 107 (+1), Day 105 (+3), Day 101 (+7), Day 87 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**From the closed form to a loss.** Day 106 showed that the KL-regularised optimum is π*(y|x) = π_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β)/Z(x). Rearranging gives the reward in terms of the policy: r(x,y) = β·log(π*(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β·log Z(x). Put that into the Bradley-Terry likelihood. Z(x) depends only on the prompt, so it cancels in r(x,y_w) − r(x,y_l), and we get the **DPO loss**:

L = −log σ( β·[ (log π_θ(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x)) − (log π_θ(y_l|x) − log π_ref(y_l|x)) ] ).

No reward model, no sampling, no value network: just two forward passes per pair through the policy, plus reference log-probs, which can be precomputed once because π_ref is frozen. The quantity β·log(π_θ/π_ref) is called the **implicit reward**. The bracket times β is the **reward margin**; "preference accuracy" is the fraction of pairs with a positive margin.

**What the gradient does.** The gradient raises log π_θ(y_w) and lowers log π_θ(y_l), weighted by σ(−margin): pairs the model already ranks correctly get little weight. Nothing in the loss requires the chosen answer to become *likely*, only *more likely relative to the rejected one than under the reference*. Lowering the rejected answer is often the easiest way to do that. This explains today's main result.

**Sequence log-probs.** log π(y|x) is the sum of the log-probs of the answer tokens given the prompt and the earlier answer tokens. The prompt tokens are masked out. In `_pref.logp_batch`, the mask starts at position prompt_len − 1 because logits at position t predict token t+1.

**Implementation choices.** Policy = SmolLM2-135M-Instruct + LoRA r=16 on all linear layers (Week 15's `_lora.py`). Reference = the same instruct model before adding LoRA. β = 0.1, lr 5e-4, 120 steps × 4 pairs, each training pair used once.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
`code/week-16/day108_dpo.py`.

**Verified (out/day108_dpo.log):**
- Reference log-probs for 480 train + 40 test pairs took 23 s. Mean reference log-prob: chosen −20.2, rejected −7.1. The instruct model finds the *wrong* (unreversed) answer far more likely.
- Before DPO: exact match 0.00. The model starts explaining ("To reverse the order of these words, I will first identify the words that are").
- Step 0: loss 0.693 (= log 2, margin 0, as expected at initialisation since LoRA B = 0).
- Step 20: loss 0.000, margin 9.87, pref acc 1.00; Δlog-prob chosen +10.7, rejected −87.9.
- Step 119: margin 12.08; Δ chosen +14.2, rejected −106.6. Training took 84 s.
- Held-out pairs: implicit-reward preference accuracy 1.00, mean margin 13.53, Δ chosen +8.6, Δ rejected −126.7.
- After DPO: exact match **0.17**. Example: "spring orange lemon cloud" → "clouds orange springfirehose".

The loss was at 0.000 from step 20, and held-out preference accuracy was perfect, yet only 0.17 of generations were exactly right and the example output is broken. DPO mostly made the rejected answer extremely unlikely (−126.7 nats) while raising the chosen one only modestly. Pushing down one specific wrong answer does not make the right answer the model's greedy output, and with a high learning rate the probability mass goes partly to garbage.

### Extra notes
DPO is *offline*: it learns from a fixed set of pairs, usually generated by some other model. Online or iterative variants regenerate pairs from the current policy. Known DPO pathologies include the one seen here (both or only the rejected log-probs fall), sensitivity to β and learning rate, and length exploitation. Variants add an NLL term on the chosen answer (tested on Day 112), normalise by length, or change the loss shape (IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO are names to look up; read their papers before relying on any claim about them).

### Interview-style question (15 min)
"Explain DPO to an engineer who knows SFT, and say when you would choose it over PPO." Points: same objective as KL-regularised RLHF; the optimal policy defines an implicit reward, so the BT loss can be written in policy log-probs; no RM or sampling loop, so it is cheap and stable; but it is offline, depends on pair quality and coverage, and can lower the chosen likelihood. PPO/online methods are preferred when you have a good reward or verifier and want the model to learn from its own samples.

- Using the policy (with gradients) as the reference.
- Averaging token log-probs in DPO but summing them for the reference (or vice versa).
- Reporting training preference accuracy as the result.

## Day 109: β, learning rate and forgetting in DPO
**Review today:** Day 108 (+1), Day 106 (+3), Day 102 (+7), Day 88 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**β in DPO.** In the RLHF objective β is the KL weight. In the DPO loss it is the scale on log-ratios inside the sigmoid. Larger β means a given log-prob change produces a bigger margin, so the loss saturates *sooner* and the gradient weight σ(−margin) drops sooner. The policy therefore moves less from π_ref. Smaller β lets the policy move further before the loss is satisfied. That is the same direction as in Day 106: big β = stay close.

**Learning rate interacts.** With LoRA at lr 5e-4 (Day 108) the optimiser can satisfy the loss in a few steps by drastic changes. Lower lr changes the path, not only the speed: a small step size finds changes closer to the reference.

**Forgetting.** Preference tuning can damage abilities that the pairs do not cover. A cheap proxy is perplexity on held-out general text. We reuse Week 15's Shakespeare validation text: 8 windows of 256 tokens, on which the instruct model scored 32.05 (Week 15 log `day104_eval_finetuned.log`).

**Measuring movement.** Three numbers tell you how far the policy moved: Δlog-prob of chosen and rejected on held-out pairs, the change in general perplexity, and sample outputs. A full KL estimate would require sampling from the policy; Δlog-probs on the pairs are a cheap substitute.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Day 108: β 0.1, lr 5e-4, 120 steps | 13.53 | +8.6 | −126.7 | 0.17 | not measured | "clouds orange springfirehose" |
| β 0.1, lr 1e-4, 60 steps | 9.19 | +10.7 | −81.2 | 0.23 | 32.35 | "clouds orange spring" |
| β 0.5, lr 1e-4, 60 steps | 11.32 | +12.3 | −10.3 | 0.20 | 32.14 | "cloud orange lemon spring" |

Perplexity barely moved from 32.05 in either case (32.35 and 32.14), so on this proxy neither run forgot much. Exact match is 0.20-0.23 on 40 prompts; Day 110 shows that such differences are within noise.

## Day 110: LLM evaluation pitfalls
**Review today:** Day 109 (+1), Day 107 (+3), Day 103 (+7), Day 89 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
An evaluation number is a measurement with error bars and assumptions. Six pitfalls cause most bad conclusions.

**1. Small samples.** With n test items and accuracy p, the standard error is about √(p(1−p)/n). On 40 items at p = 0.2 that is about 0.06, so differences of 0.03-0.06 are noise. Report a confidence interval; the bootstrap (resample the per-item results with replacement many times) is simple and assumption-light.

**2. Prompt sensitivity.** Small models, and sometimes large ones, change their answers when the instruction is reworded, the casing changes or punctuation is removed. One prompt template is one sample from a distribution of reasonable prompts. Report several, or fix the template and say so.

**3. Metric choice.** Exact match, normalised match (lowercase, strip punctuation) and "contains the answer" can give different numbers for the same outputs. "Contains" can reward a model that outputs many candidates. Choose the metric before looking at results and report how it was computed.

**4. Contamination.** If test items (or close variants) appear in training data, scores measure memorisation. Check exact overlap and n-gram overlap. For public benchmarks and web-trained models you usually *cannot* fully check, which is why fresh, private or dynamically generated tests matter.

**5. Judge bias.** LLM judges have position, length and self-preference biases. Week 15 measured this: the 135M instruct judge gave 0 of 16 consistent verdicts when the two answers were swapped.

**6. Optimising the metric.** Once a number is a target, models and people tune toward it (Goodhart's law). The Day 107 RM shortcut and the Day 111 reward exploitation are versions of this.

## Day 111: Best-of-n, verifiers and exploiting an implicit reward
**Review today:** Day 110 (+1), Day 108 (+3), Day 104 (+7), Day 90 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Best-of-n (rejection sampling).** Sample n answers, score each, return the best. It is the simplest way to "optimise" against a reward without changing weights. Its strength depends on two things: whether the right answer appears among the samples at all (*pass@n*), and whether the scorer can recognise it.

**Scorers.** (a) The policy's own log-prob: picks the answer the model already likes most; close to greedy. (b) A learned reward model: works if the RM is accurate on these samples. (c) A *verifier*: for tasks with checkable answers (unit tests, exact arithmetic, word reversal) a program can decide correctness. With a perfect verifier, best-of-n accuracy equals pass@n.

**DPO's implicit reward as a scorer.** DPO defines r̂(x,y) = β·log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)). It looks like a free reward model. But it was only trained to rank the chosen/rejected kinds of answers. On other samples, especially strange ones the reference finds very unlikely, the ratio can be huge for a bad answer. That is reward hacking at inference time.

**Best-of-n and RLHF.** Best-of-n is also used to build training data (keep the best samples and fine-tune on them: "rejection sampling fine-tuning", or "expert iteration"), and as a baseline: an RL method should beat best-of-n at the same compute.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
`code/week-16/day111_best_of_n.py`: for each of the 40 test prompts, sample 8 answers from the Day 108 DPO model at temperature 0.8 and choose by each scorer. The implicit reward uses β = 0.1; the reference log-probs come from the same model with the LoRA scale set to 0. Run time 54 s.

**Verified (out/day111_best_of_n.log):**
- Greedy: 0.17.
- Best-of-8 by policy log-prob: 0.15.
- Best-of-8 by DPO implicit reward: **0.00**.
- Oracle verifier (pass@8): 0.20.
- Mean distinct samples per prompt: 5.4 of 8.

The verifier gains only a little over greedy (0.17 → 0.20), because the correct answer rarely appears among 8 samples: this policy is weak, so selection cannot help much. Choosing by the implicit reward never picked a correct answer, even on prompts where the verifier found one among the samples (random choice was not measured). The samples it picks are ones the reference model finds extremely unlikely, which the log-ratio rewards. A scorer outside its training distribution is exploited by optimisation, even one as mild as picking 1 of 8.

(3) It is a log-ratio; any answer the reference rates very unlikely gets a big value if the policy rates it less unlikely, whether or not it is good. ### Extra notes

Verifier-based methods (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) are a major current direction exactly because verifiers do not drift like learned RMs; but they exist only for some tasks.

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**The question.** Given preference pairs, is it better to (a) do SFT on the chosen answers only, (b) do DPO, or (c) do DPO plus an NLL (SFT) term on the chosen answer? Option (c) is a common fix for DPO lowering the chosen likelihood: the NLL term explicitly keeps the chosen answer probable.

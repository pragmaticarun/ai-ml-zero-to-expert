## Day 99: Hugging Face transformers and datasets
**Review today:** Day 98 (+1), Day 96 (+3), Day 92 (+7), Day 78 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**The Hub.** The Hugging Face Hub hosts model repositories: a `config.json` (architecture), weights (`model.safetensors`), tokenizer files, and a model card (license, training data, intended use, limitations). Always read the card and the license before use.

**The three objects.**
- `AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)`: text ↔ token ids, plus special tokens and the chat template.
- `AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name)`: picks the right class from `config.json` (SmolLM2 is a Llama-architecture model, so you get `LlamaForCausalLM`).
- `model.generate(...)`: the decoding loop (greedy, sampling, beam search), with the KV cache handled for you.
`from_pretrained` downloads into the cache (`HF_HOME`) once; later loads are local. Setting `HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1` guarantees no network access, which makes runs reproducible.

**What is inside SmolLM2-135M.** It is the Week 14 GPT grown up: pre-norm blocks (RMSNorm), RoPE positions (Day 93), SwiGLU MLPs, tied embeddings, and **grouped-query attention** (GQA): 9 query heads share 3 key/value heads, which shrinks the KV cache 3× (Day 111).

**Forward pass and loss.** `model(input_ids=x, labels=x)` returns `.loss`, the mean next-token cross-entropy. The library shifts labels internally, so you pass the *same* tensor as input and labels. Positions with label −100 are ignored (Day 103 uses this to mask prompts).

**`datasets`.** The `datasets` library loads Hub datasets as memory-mapped Arrow tables with `.map`, `.filter` and streaming. This week our data is small and local, so we tokenise plain tensors; the concepts transfer.

## Day 100: Fine-tuning a small pretrained model
**Review today:** Day 99 (+1), Day 97 (+3), Day 93 (+7), Day 79 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Why fine-tune?** Pretraining on trillions of tokens is far beyond our budget. A pretrained model already knows language; fine-tuning on a small domain corpus moves it towards that domain's style and vocabulary. This is transfer learning (Day 75) for language models.

**Full fine-tuning** updates every weight with the same next-token loss used in pretraining, at a much smaller learning rate (around 1e-5 to 1e-4 rather than ~1e-3), so the model does not destroy what it knows.

**Memory.** With AdamW in fp32, each parameter needs 4 bytes (weight) + 4 (gradient) + 8 (two Adam moments) = 16 bytes, plus activations. For 134.5M parameters that is about **2.0 GiB** before activations. For a 7B model it would be about 112 GB, which is why full fine-tuning of large models needs several GPUs, mixed precision, and sharded optimiser states (ZeRO / FSDP), and why Day 101's LoRA exists.

**Risks.**
- *Catastrophic forgetting:* the model gets better on the new domain and worse elsewhere (measured on Day 105).
- *Over-fitting* small datasets after a few epochs.
- *Learning-rate sensitivity:* too high wrecks the pretrained features quickly.

**Recipe.** Small learning rate, gradient clipping, a short warmup, evaluation on a held-out split before and after, and a probe set from the *original* domain to measure forgetting.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
Run `code/week-15/day100_full_finetune.py` (**96 s**). Settings: 100 steps, batches of 4 × 128 tokens, AdamW lr 5e-5, clip 1.0. **Verified:**
- 134,515,008 parameters; fp32 weights + gradients + Adam ≈ **2.00 GiB**.
- Validation perplexity (8 windows): **26.29 → 21.38** after 100 steps (0.88 s/step), from only 51,200 training tokens.
- Training loss is noisy because each batch is only 512 tokens: 3.671 at step 0, 3.005 at step 40, 3.748 at step 90, 3.073 at step 99. Use the validation number, not single-batch training losses.
- Sample after fine-tuning (prompt "ROMEO:"): "I will find you again. LADY CAPULET: That's well: 'tis not I, as I have been heard say, That 'tis a common thing". Compare with the Day 95 model: real words, grammatical lines, a correct character name from the same play.

### Extra notes: gradient checkpointing
Activations for a long sequence through 30 layers can exceed the weights' memory. Gradient checkpointing stores only some layer inputs and recomputes the rest during the backward pass, trading about a third more compute for much less memory. In transformers it is `model.gradient_checkpointing_enable()`.

**How much data is enough?** Here 100 steps used 51,200 tokens, about one sixth of the 305,111-token training split, and perplexity still fell by 19%. Domain style is cheap to learn because the pretrained model already has the words; the fine-tune mostly shifts probabilities toward archaic forms, verse line breaks and speaker tags. New *facts* are much harder to add by fine-tuning and are better supplied at inference time with retrieval (Day 108).

## Day 101: LoRA from scratch, then PEFT
**Review today:** Day 100 (+1), Day 98 (+3), Day 94 (+7), Day 80 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Low-rank adaptation** (Hu et al., 2021, "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models"). Freeze the pretrained weight W (d_out × d_in). Learn an update ΔW = B·A with A of shape r × d_in and B of shape d_out × r, with rank r much smaller than d. The layer computes

  y = W x + (α / r) · B A x.

- **Initialisation:** A random, B zero, so ΔW = 0 and training starts exactly at the pretrained model.
- **Scale α / r:** keeps the update size comparable across ranks, so you can change r without retuning the learning rate much.
- **Parameters:** r·(d_in + d_out) per adapted matrix instead of d_in·d_out.
- **Merging:** after training, W' = W + (α/r)·B A gives a plain model with zero inference overhead. Or keep adapters separate and swap them per task on one shared base.

**Why it works.** Hu et al. report that the weight updates needed for adaptation have low "intrinsic rank": a small r captures most of the benefit for many tasks. It is not guaranteed for every task; tasks that need much new knowledge benefit from higher r or full fine-tuning.

**Memory benefit.** Gradients and Adam states are needed only for A and B. The frozen W can even be stored in lower precision (Day 102). The forward and backward activations are still needed, so LoRA saves optimiser memory more than activation memory.

**PEFT.** The `peft` library wraps a model: `get_peft_model(model, LoraConfig(r, lora_alpha, target_modules, lora_dropout))`, and offers `merge_and_unload()`, saving adapters only, and many other methods (prefix tuning, IA³ and others).

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
Run `code/week-15/day101_lora.py` (**119 s**). LoRA r = 8, α = 16 on q_proj and v_proj in all 30 layers, lr 1e-3, 100 steps of 4 × 128 tokens. **Verified:**
- Trainable **460,800** of 134,975,808 (**0.34%**). Check: per layer, q (576→576) adds 8·(576+576) = 9,216 and v (576→192) adds 8·(576+192) = 6,144, so 15,360 × 30 = 460,800.
- Logits change at init (B = 0): **0.0e+00**. The adapted model is exactly the base model before training.
- My LoRA: **0.47 s/step** (vs 0.88 for full fine-tuning), validation perplexity **26.29 → 23.19**.
- Merging into W: max logit difference **7.4e-05** (float32 rounding), and the parameter count returns to 134,515,008.
- `peft` with the same config: trainable **460,800**, equal to mine (**True**); after 100 steps with the same seed, validation perplexity **23.16** (mine 23.19). `merge_and_unload()` returns a plain `LlamaForCausalLM`.
- Compare Day 100: full fine-tuning reached 21.38 with the same steps. LoRA on q,v recovered about 60% of that improvement ((26.29 − 23.19)/(26.29 − 21.38) ≈ 0.63) with 0.34% of the trainable parameters. Day 105 tests more ranks and targets.

The original paper adapted attention matrices. Later practice (for example in QLoRA, Day 102) adapts all linear layers including the MLP, which often helps more than raising r on attention alone. Day 105 tests exactly this trade-off at CPU scale.

"Explain LoRA to a colleague and when you would not use it." Frozen W plus a trainable low-rank update; cheap, swappable, mergeable. Avoid when the task needs large new knowledge or when you can afford full fine-tuning and want the last bit of quality.

## Day 102: PEFT configurations and QLoRA concepts
**Review today:** Day 101 (+1), Day 99 (+3), Day 95 (+7), Day 81 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Choosing a LoRA configuration.** Three knobs: rank r, scale α, and target modules. For a Llama-style block the linear layers are q, k, v, o (attention) and gate, up, down (SwiGLU MLP). Adapting more modules adds parameters roughly linearly; raising r does too.

**QLoRA** (Dettmers et al., 2023, "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"). Key ideas:
1. Store the frozen base in **4-bit** (their NF4 data type, designed for normally distributed weights), with **double quantisation** of the scale factors.
2. Dequantise blocks on the fly to bf16 for each matmul.
3. Train LoRA adapters (in bf16) on **all linear layers**.
4. Use **paged optimisers** to survive memory spikes.
The paper reports fine-tuning a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU while matching 16-bit fine-tuning quality on their benchmarks.

**Why it works.** Gradients flow *through* the quantised weights to the adapters, but the quantised weights are never updated. The adapters can partly compensate for quantisation error, because they are trained with the quantised base in the loop.

**Group-wise quantisation in one paragraph** (Day 110 goes deeper). Split a weight matrix into groups of g values. For each group store a scale s = max|w| / 7 and integers q = round(w / s) clipped to [−8, 7] (4 bits). Dequantise as q·s. Memory: half a byte per weight plus a 16-bit scale per group (for g = 64, 0.53 bytes per weight vs 4 for fp32).

**CPU honesty.** The bitsandbytes 4-bit kernels used by QLoRA in practice target GPUs. Here we **simulate** 4-bit: we replace each frozen weight with its quantise-dequantise round trip (the same numerical error, but stored in fp32, so no memory is saved on this box). This shows the quality effects, not the memory savings.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
Run `code/week-15/day102_peft_qlora.py` (**43 s**). **Verified:**

Trainable parameters with `peft` (of 134,515,008):

|---|---|---|
| q, v | 230,400 (0.17%) | 921,600 (0.69%) |
| q, k, v, o | 460,800 (0.34%) | 1,843,200 (1.37%) |
| all 7 linear | 1,221,120 (0.91%) | 4,884,480 (3.63%) |

- Their memory: fp32 **405 MiB**, bf16 **202 MiB**, int4 with an fp16 scale per 64 **54 MiB**.
- Validation perplexity: fp32 base **26.29**, 4-bit base **36.89**. Naive round-to-nearest 4-bit hurts a small model noticeably (larger models usually tolerate it better, and better methods such as NF4, GPTQ and AWQ lose less; Day 110).

## Day 103: Instruction tuning and chat templates
**Review today:** Day 102 (+1), Day 100 (+3), Day 96 (+7), Day 82 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Base vs instruct models.** A base model continues text. Ask it a question and it may continue with more questions, as Day 99's greedy loop showed. An *instruct* (chat) model has been further trained to answer. Instruction tuning is the first step of that post-training.

**Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** Collect (instruction, response) pairs and fine-tune with the next-token loss on the formatted conversation. Wei et al. (2022), "Finetuned Language Models Are Zero-Shot Learners" (FLAN), and Ouyang et al. (2022), "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (InstructGPT), established the recipe: SFT on demonstrations, then preference optimisation (Day 106).

**Chat templates.** Conversations are serialised with special tokens. SmolLM2-Instruct uses the ChatML style: `<|im_start|>system … <|im_end|>`, `<|im_start|>user … <|im_end|>`, `<|im_start|>assistant`. The template lives in the tokenizer (`tokenizer.apply_chat_template`). Use the *exact* template the model was trained with; a mismatch silently degrades quality. `add_generation_prompt=True` appends the assistant header so the model starts answering.

**Prompt-loss masking.** Usually only the response tokens are trained: set the labels of system and user tokens to −100. Arguments for it: the model should learn to answer, not to generate user questions; and long prompts would otherwise dominate the loss. Arguments against: for short prompts it may matter little, and some work finds that training on prompts also helps. We test it.

**Stop tokens.** The response must end with `<|im_end|>` in training, and generation must stop on that token. Our first run forgot to pass it as an EOS token to `generate`: the model produced the right words and then rambled ("window tiger coffeeaidaidaid"), scoring 0.07. A one-line fix (`eos_token_id=[…, im_end]`) is the difference between a broken and a working evaluation.

**Our task.** Reversing the order of 3-5 words from a 40-word list ("Reverse the order of these words: red fish blue" → "blue fish red"). It is synthetic, automatically checkable, and something a 135M base model cannot do zero-shot.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
Run `code/week-15/day103_instruction_tuning.py` (about 6 minutes). 2,000 training examples; 40 held-out prompts not in the training set; LoRA r = 16 on all linear layers, lr 1e-3, 250 steps of 8 examples. **Verified:**
- The rendered template contains a default system prompt ("You are a helpful AI assistant named SmolLM, trained by Hugging Face"), then the user turn, then `<|im_start|>assistant`.
- One example is **48** tokens, of which only **6** (the answer plus `<|im_end|>`) count in the masked loss.
- Zero-shot exact match on the 40 held-out prompts: base **0.00**, SmolLM2-135M-Instruct **0.00**.
- After SFT with prompt masking: exact match **1.00** (135 s). Sample: "coffee tiger window" → **"window tiger coffee"**.
- After SFT *without* masking: also **1.00** (132 s).

So on this task the masking choice made no difference: the prompt is highly regular, and 250 steps were enough either way. Report it as a null result for this setting, not as evidence that masking never matters. Harder tasks with varied, long prompts are where masking is usually argued to matter.

## Day 104: Evaluating LLMs and the fine-tuned model
**Review today:** Day 103 (+1), Day 101 (+3), Day 97 (+7), Day 83 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Evaluate on several axes.** A fine-tune is a trade. Measure:
1. **The target task**, with an exact metric where possible (exact match, unit tests, F1).
2. **Retention:** perplexity or tasks from the original domain, to detect forgetting.
3. **General ability:** standard benchmarks.

**Perplexity** is cheap and sensitive but only comparable with the same tokenizer and text, and it does not measure helpfulness.

**Multiple-choice benchmarks** (MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC and others) are often scored by **log-likelihood**: compute log P(option | question) for each option and pick the highest. Choices matter: summing token log-probs penalises long options; length normalisation (mean per token) or per-character normalisation counteracts that. Different harnesses make different choices, so the same model can get different scores. Always report how it was scored.

**Generative evaluation** checks free-form answers by exact match, by execution (code), or by a judge.

**LLM-as-a-judge** (Zheng et al., 2023, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena") uses a strong model to grade or compare answers. It scales, but it has known biases: **position bias** (preferring the first or second answer), **verbosity bias**, and **self-preference**. Mitigation: evaluate both orders and count only consistent verdicts, use rubrics, and check agreement with humans on a sample.

**Contamination.** Benchmark questions leak into web-scale pretraining data, which inflates scores. Prefer held-out or freshly written tests for decisions that matter.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
Run `code/week-15/day104_eval_finetuned.py` (**35 s**; needs the Day 103 adapter). Models: base, SmolLM2-135M-Instruct, and base + the Day 103 SFT adapter (loaded with 0 unexpected keys). **Verified:**

| | base | instruct | base + SFT LoRA |
|---|---|---|---|
| reverse-words exact match (40) | 0.00 | 0.00 | **1.00** |
| Shakespeare val perplexity | 26.29 | 32.05 | **57.90** |
| 16-question MC, sum log-prob | 0.88 | 0.88 | 0.88 |
| 16-question MC, length-normalised | 0.81 | 0.81 | 0.81 |

- **Forgetting is real:** the SFT adapter more than doubled Shakespeare perplexity (26.29 → 57.90) while learning the task perfectly. The instruct model is also worse than the base at plain text continuation (32.05), a common side-effect of chat post-training.
- **The small MC set did not separate the models.** All three got the same scores. With 16 questions, one question is 6 percentage points, so this set is too small to detect anything but huge differences. Length normalisation *lowered* accuracy here (0.81 vs 0.88), so it is not automatically better. The questions were written for this course (basic facts, 4 options), not taken from a benchmark.
- **Position bias, demonstrated.** Using the instruct model as a judge ("Which statement is true? A … B …"), on 16 pairs in both orders: it chose A in **10 of 32** cases, gave the *same* verdict after swapping in **0 of 16** pairs (always the same letter regardless of content), and was correct **16 of 32** times, exactly chance. A 135M model is far too weak to judge, and this is the failure mode the swap test exists to catch.

## Day 105: PROJECT W15: full fine-tune vs LoRA; Quiz 15
**Review today:** Day 104 (+1), Day 102 (+3), Day 98 (+7), Day 84 (+21; quick 15-min skim of its self-check).

### Block 1: Concept lesson (1h15)
**Project question.** With the same data and number of steps, how do full fine-tuning and LoRA variants compare in target quality, trainable parameters, time and forgetting?

**Design.** One script (`day105_project_lora.py`), one seed, Shakespeare as the target domain, and **this course's own notes** (`weeks/week-01.md`, 12,705 SmolLM tokens) as the forgetting probe: modern technical English, very different from Shakespeare. Same 80 steps of 4 × 128 tokens for every run. Full fine-tuning at lr 5e-5 (Day 100); LoRA at lr 1e-3 (Day 101).

**Confounders to keep in mind.** The learning rates differ (each is a typical value for its method), and there is one seed. So the comparison is "typical settings", not "equal settings". A fair study would tune the learning rate per method and average seeds.

**Deliverables.** The results table, a 300-word report with a recommendation, and Quiz 15.

### Block 2: Worked example (45 min)
Run `code/week-15/day105_project_lora.py` (**250 s** total). **Verified:**

| run | trainable | Shakespeare val ppl | notes ppl | time (80 steps) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no training | 0 | 26.29 | 24.89 | — |
| full fine-tune, lr 5e-5 | 134,515,008 | **21.34** | 26.29 | 59 s |
| LoRA q,v r = 2 | 115,200 | 23.23 | 27.11 | 48 s |
| LoRA q,v r = 8 | 460,800 | 23.02 | 28.29 | 35 s |
| LoRA q,v r = 32 | 1,843,200 | 22.68 | 30.17 | 38 s |
| LoRA all-linear r = 8 | 2,442,240 | 21.42 | 28.65 | 51 s |

Findings:
- **Target quality:** full fine-tuning was best (21.34), with LoRA on all linear layers nearly equal (21.42) using 1.8% of the trainable parameters. On q,v only, higher rank helped modestly (23.23 → 22.68 from r = 2 to 32). *Where* you adapt mattered more than rank: all-linear r = 8 beat q,v r = 32 with a similar parameter count.
- **Forgetting:** every run got worse on the course notes. Here full fine-tuning forgot *least* (24.89 → 26.29), and LoRA forgetting grew with rank (27.11 → 30.17). That is the opposite of the common intuition that LoRA forgets less; Biderman et al. (2024), "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less", report that pattern at much larger scale. The likely cause here is the learning rate: LoRA ran at 1e-3, 20× higher than full fine-tuning. This is why the confounder note above matters.
- **Time:** LoRA steps were faster (35-51 s vs 59 s) but not dramatically, because the forward and backward passes through the frozen model still dominate on CPU. Timings varied between runs (r = 2 took longer than r = 8), so do not over-read them.
